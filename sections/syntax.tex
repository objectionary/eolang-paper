\section{Syntax}\label{sec:syntax}

EO syntax is presented line by line.
Every non-blank, non-comment line falls into exactly one \emph{line shape}
  --- a syntactic category determined by the first non-space character of
  the line and a small lookahead.
Multi-line constructs are built by composition:
  a head line at one indent, followed by a deeper-indent block of children
  and possibly one or more same-indent continuation lines.

This section catalogues the line shapes first (\cref{sec:syntax:lines}),
  then the multi-line constructs that wrap them (\cref{sec:syntax:multi-line}),
  then the name suffixes that may close a head line (\cref{sec:syntax:names}).
The cross-line rules that decide which compositions are legal are deferred
  to \cref{sec:wellformedness}.
The complete enumeration of outer expression kinds with their structural
  properties is given in \cref{app:outer-kinds}.

\subsubsection{Meta directive}\label{sec:syntax:meta}

A meta directive has the form
  \ff{+name part\(_1\) part\(_2\) \ldots}.
The name immediately follows the \ff{+}; each part is a maximal run of
  non-whitespace characters separated from its neighbours by exactly one space.
Parts may contain \ff{:}, \ff{.}, \ff{-}, and \ff{/} ---
  for example \ff{+rt jvm a.b.c:lib:1.0.0}.

Meta directives are legal only at indent 0 and only before any other object
  has been declared in the source.
The catalogue of recognised directive names
  (\ff{+architect}, \ff{+version}, \ff{+package}, \ff{+alias}, \ff{+rt}, \ldots)
  with their per-name argument shapes is given in \cref{sec:sem:meta}.

A leading \ff{+} followed by a digit is not a meta directive ---
  it is a signed-number literal opening an application line
  (\cref{sec:syntax:app}).
A \ff{+}-digit token whose digit-run does not form a valid \ff{INT} or
  \ff{FLOAT} (e.g.\ \ff{+1foo} where the digit-run is followed by an
  identifier letter with no intervening separator) is a lexical error;
  the parser does not silently treat it as a meta directive.

\subsubsection{Comment line}\label{sec:syntax:comment}

A line whose first non-space character is \ff{\#} is a comment line.
The \ff{\#} is followed by the rest of the line, which may contain any
  characters except line terminators.
Consecutive comment lines at the same indent form a \emph{comment block}.
Comment blocks attach to the named object that follows them
  (\cref{sec:wf:comments}).
The cross-line rules of comment-block attachment, dangling comments, and
  blank-line separation are given in \cref{sec:wellformedness}.

\subsubsection{Formation line}\label{sec:syntax:formation}

A formation line opens with \ff{[}.
The bracketed list enumerates zero or more \emph{void parameters} ---
  unbound attribute slots whose values are supplied by the caller at
  application time.
A void parameter name is a \ff{NAME} token or the \ff{PHI} token (\ff{@}).
The \ff{RHO} token (\ff{\textasciicircum{}}) is rejected as a parameter name.

The list contents must satisfy:
  no leading or trailing space inside the brackets,
  and exactly one space between parameter names.
The closing \ff{]} may be followed by one of the optional name suffixes
  catalogued in \cref{sec:syntax:names}.

\begin{ffcode}
[a b] > foo            // formation with two voids, named foo
[] > bar               // formation with no voids, named bar
[a b] > foo /number    // atom declaration (Section 3.3)
\end{ffcode}

The outer kind of a formation line is \ff{bare-formation}.
After the head line, a deeper-indent block of children may follow;
  the body's syntactic role is described in \cref{sec:syntax:vapp}
  and its well-formedness in \cref{sec:wf:naming,sec:wf:atoms}.

\subsubsection{Method-dispatch line}\label{sec:syntax:method}

A line whose first non-space character is \ff{.} is a method-dispatch line.
The leading dot consumes the dispatch position, and the method name follows.
Inside a method-dispatch line the special names \ff{@} and \ff{\textasciicircum}
  retain their token identities (\ff{PHI}, \ff{RHO}) and translate to
  \(\varphi\) and \(\rho\) respectively (\cref{sec:semantics}).

After the method name, zero or more horizontal arguments may follow,
  separated by single spaces, and an optional name suffix may close the line.
A method-dispatch line is always a continuation of an earlier expression
  at the same indent: it has no meaning standing alone at top level,
  appearing as a deeper child of an unrelated parent, or following an
  expression that is already closed for further continuation.
The three illegal positions are spelled out in \cref{sec:wf:notation}.

The outer kind a method-dispatch line ultimately produces depends on whether
  it carries horizontal arguments.
With zero hargs the chain stays \ff{vmethod} and admits further continuations;
  with one or more hargs the chain becomes \ff{vmethod-with-hargs} and is
  closed for further extension (\cref{app:outer-kinds}).

\subsubsection{Application line}\label{sec:syntax:app}

An application line has the form \ff{head arg\(_1\) arg\(_2\) \ldots [suffix]}
  with a head expression followed by zero or more horizontal arguments.
The head is one of:

\begin{itemize}
  \item a paren group \ff{( \ldots )};
  \item a data literal --- a \ff{STRING}, \ff{INT}, \ff{FLOAT}, \ff{HEX},
    or \ff{BYTES}, optionally followed by a method chain;
  \item a star tuple \ff{*};
  \item a bare identifier (a \ff{NAME}, \ff{ROOT}, or \ff{XI}),
    optionally followed by a chain of \ff{.method} segments on the same line.
\end{itemize}

Each argument is itself a small dispatch:
  a paren group, a literal optionally followed by a method chain,
  a \ff{*} tuple, or an identifier head with optional method chain.
Arguments are separated by exactly one space.
An argument may carry an inline binding \ff{:label} or \ff{:N}
  (\cref{sec:syntax:binding}), subject to the all-or-nothing rule of
  \cref{sec:wf:bindings}.

The classifier of an application line ignores characters that live inside
  parentheses or string literals: only top-level spaces and dots determine the
  shape.

A \emph{horizontal formation} of the form \ff{[x]} or \ff{[x] body}
  is \textbf{not} a legal horizontal argument.
A formation may appear only as the top-of-line construct
  (\cref{sec:syntax:formation}), as a vertical argument at deeper indent,
  or as the parameter list of an inline-phi formation
  (\cref{sec:syntax:inline-phi}).

\begin{ffcode}
foo                          // head only
foo.bar.baz                  // method chain, 0 hargs
foo a b c                    // head + 3 horizontal args
foo.bar 42 "x"               // chained head + 2 hargs
(foo bar).baz x              // chained head over a paren group + 1 harg
\end{ffcode}

The outer kind produced depends on chain length and harg count:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Head only, no chain, no hargs: \ff{head}.
    A bare \ff{*} in head position is also \ff{head}, but its semantic
    expansion is to a \(\Phi.\mathtt{tuple}\)
    (\cref{sec:sem:tuple}).
  \item Head with chained \ff{.method} segments and 0 hargs: \ff{hmethod}.
  \item Head with one or more horizontal arguments: \ff{happlication},
    regardless of whether the head itself is chained.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Reversed dispatch line}\label{sec:syntax:reversed}

A line whose lead identifier ends with a trailing \ff{.} followed by a space
  or end-of-line is a \emph{reversed dispatch}.
The identifier preceding the dot must be a single \ff{NAME}, \ff{PHI}
  (\ff{@}), or \ff{RHO} (\ff{\textasciicircum}) token ---
  no dotted paths, no \ff{ROOT}, no literal-rooted prefixes.
A trailing dot after a chain such as \ff{Q.x.foo.} is therefore not a
  reversed dispatch but a lexical error.

Two surface forms exist, and they are mutually exclusive on any one line.

\paragraph{Horizontal form.}
\ff{name. recv arg\(_1\) arg\(_2\) \ldots} ---
  the first horizontal argument is the receiver of the dispatch,
  the remaining horizontal arguments are method arguments of \ff{.name}.
The line is closed for any deeper-indent continuation.
Inline bindings on arguments follow the rules of \cref{sec:wf:bindings};
  in particular the receiver itself may not carry a binding.
The outer kind is \ff{reversed-with-hargs}.

\paragraph{Vertical form.}
\ff{name.} alone, with no horizontal arguments on the head line ---
  the next non-blank line at indent \(+2\) is the receiver,
  and subsequent siblings at that indent are method arguments.
The first deeper line must not itself begin with \ff{.}.
The outer kind is \ff{bare-reversed}.

\begin{ffcode}
if. > @                    // vertical bare-reversed, named @
  cond                     //   receiver
  then                     //   method arg 1
  else                     //   method arg 2

if. cond then else         // horizontal form: receiver=cond, args=then,else

if. false 1:a 2:b > second // horizontal form, all-bound args
\end{ffcode}

A reversed-dispatch line in vertical form may carry the compact-tuple suffix
  \ff{*N} (\cref{sec:syntax:compact}); for that case \(N\geq 1\) is required.

\subsubsection{Compact tuple line}\label{sec:syntax:compact}

The compact-tuple suffix \ff{*N} (with \(N\) defaulting to \(0\))
  attaches to a head expression and partitions its vertical children:
  the first \(N\) children are direct positional arguments of the head,
  and the remaining children are collected into a single
  \(\Phi.\mathtt{tuple}\) argument appended at the end.

The head of a compact-tuple line is one of:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \ff{head}-shape expression (bare identifier, \ff{*}, or literal);
  \item an \ff{hmethod} chain with 0 horizontal arguments,
    e.g.\ \ff{foo.bar *};
  \item a \ff{bare-reversed} dispatch, e.g.\ \ff{name. *N}.
\end{itemize}

Heads that already carry horizontal arguments (\ff{happlication} or
  \ff{reversed-with-hargs}) cannot also carry \ff{*N} ---
  the compact-tuple marker comes after the head and before any arguments.

If the head is a bare-reversed dispatch, \(N\geq 1\) is required:
  the receiver occupies one direct slot,
  so \ff{name. *} (defaulting to \(N=0\)) and \ff{name. *0} are rejected.
For non-reversed heads, \(N=0\) is the default and legal:
  the line \ff{seq * > @} followed by deeper children tuples every child
  into a single \(\Phi.\mathtt{tuple}\) argument.

\begin{ffcode}
sprintf * > first         // N=0, all children tupled
  x
  y

sprintf *1 > second       // N=1, first child direct, remainder tupled
  x
  y
  z

joined. *1 > sixth        // reversed-form compact tuple, N>=1
  text ""                 //   receiver (the one direct slot)
  x                       //   tuple element
  y                       //   tuple element
\end{ffcode}

If the body of a compact-tuple line carries fewer than \(N\) children,
  the line is rejected.

\subsubsection{Text-block opener}\label{sec:syntax:text}

A line consisting of \ff{"""} alone opens a triple-quoted text block.
Body lines must be indented at least as deep as the opening line, and
  the block closes when a line containing \ff{"""} alone appears at the
  opening indent.
After the closing \ff{"""}, a method chain is permitted on the same line
  (\ff{""".as-bytes}), followed by an optional name suffix.

The body of a text block is a lexical literal, not a sequence of EO
  expressions: its content is the body lines joined by newline with the
  opening indent stripped (\cref{sec:lexical:strings}).

\begin{ffcode}
"""
hello
world""" > text-value
\end{ffcode}

An unclosed text block is reported at end-of-stream as a recoverable error.

\subsubsection{Inline binding}\label{sec:syntax:binding}

After an argument in an application or reversed-dispatch line, the suffix
  \ff{:label} (with \ff{label} a \ff{NAME}) or \ff{:N} (with \ff{N} a digit)
  declares an explicit attribute binding for the argument.
The form \ff{:label} attaches the argument to the named attribute;
  the form \ff{:N} attaches it to the \(N\)th positional slot
  (\(\alpha_0\), \(\alpha_1\), \ldots).

Inline binding is forbidden inside a formation's body:
  plain children of a formation carry their name via the name suffix,
  not via inline binding (\cref{sec:wf:naming}).
A same-indent group of arguments must obey the all-or-nothing binding rule
  of \cref{sec:wf:bindings}.

\subsection{Name Suffixes}\label{sec:syntax:names}

A line carrying an EO object name closes with a \emph{name suffix}.
There are four base forms (mutually exclusive on any one line)
  and two composite forms that wrap a base form to introduce an
  \emph{inline-phi} formation.

\paragraph{Base forms.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \ff{> name} --- an explicit name binding.
    A trailing \ff{!} (\ff{> name!}) marks the name as a constant
    (\cref{sec:sem:special}).
    An optional \ff{/sig} (\ff{> name /sig}) declares the object as an
    \emph{atom} with the given return signature (\cref{sec:wf:atoms}).
  \item \ff{>>} --- an auto-generated name.
    Auto-names are deterministic; their exact format is the prefix
    \ff{U+1F335} (cactus) followed by line and column coordinates of the
    suffix's source location, separated by \ff{-}.
    The cactus exclusion in \ff{NAME} (\cref{sec:lexical:tokens}) makes
    auto-names disjoint from user-defined names.
    A trailing \ff{!} (\ff{>>!}) is permitted but rare;
    \ff{>>} with a \ff{/sig} signature is rejected (an auto-named atom is
    not meaningful).
  \item \ff{+> name} --- a test attribute.
    Test attributes are legal only at indent level 1 of a top-level object
    (\cref{sec:wf:atoms}).
    The \ff{name} must be a \ff{NAME} token; \ff{+> @} is rejected.
  \item (none) --- no suffix.
    The line is anonymous.
    Permitted unless the object is a plain child of a formation
    (\cref{sec:wf:naming}).
\end{itemize}

The combinations \ff{> name! /sig} and \ff{>> /sig} are both rejected
  (const-marker and atom signature cannot combine, and an auto-named atom
  is forbidden).

\paragraph{Inline-phi composite forms.}
The composite form \ff{> [params] > name} introduces an
  \emph{inline-phi formation}:
  the left-hand side of the leftmost \ff{> [} becomes the \(\varphi\)-bound
  body of a fresh formation, and the right-hand side names the formation.
The auto-named variant \ff{> [params] >>} replaces the right-hand
  \ff{> name} with \ff{>>}; the auto-name attaches to the inline formation.

\begin{ffcode}
right x.neg > [x] > left              // explicit name
f.put (f.as-number.plus 1) > [s] >>   // auto-named inline formation
"x" > [a b] > pair                    // multi-parameter inline formation
\end{ffcode}

The left-hand side is horizontal by construction: the entire body must fit
  on the line that carries the suffix.
The line is closed for deeper-indent children ---
  the body lives in the left-hand expression, not in a vertical block.
Allowed left-hand kinds are \ff{head}, \ff{hmethod}, \ff{happlication},
  and a paren group; a \ff{reversed-with-hargs} line or a horizontal
  formation may not appear as the left-hand side.

Exactly one \ff{> [params]} suffix may appear per line:
  chained forms such as \ff{expr > [a] > [b] > name} are rejected.
The right-hand side must carry a name --- bare \ff{> [params]} with
  nothing after the parameter list is rejected.
A test attribute may not stand at the right-hand side of inline-phi:
  \ff{> [params] +> name} is rejected.

\paragraph{Atom signature.}
The signature \ff{sig} in the atom form \ff{> name /sig} is a single
  \ff{NAME} or a dotted path of names, optionally rooted at \ff{Q}
  (mapped to \(\Phi\) in the semantic translation):

\begin{align*}
\mathit{sig}\;\;::=\;\;& \mathit{NAME}\;(\,\mathtt{.}\;\mathit{NAME}\,)^{*}\\
\;\;|\;\;& \mathtt{Q}\;(\,\mathtt{.}\;\mathit{NAME}\,)^{+}
\end{align*}

A bare \ff{Q} alone (with no dot-name) is rejected as a signature;
  the signature must end in a \ff{NAME}.

\subsection{Multi-line Constructs}\label{sec:syntax:multi-line}

Multi-line constructs are built by composition.
A head line at indent \(K\) admits zero or more deeper-indent child lines
  at indent \(K+2\), and zero or more same-indent continuation lines at
  indent \(K\), subject to the per-kind admissibility rules of
  \cref{app:outer-kinds} and the cross-line rules of \cref{sec:wf:notation}.

\subsubsection{Vertical application}\label{sec:syntax:vapp}

A head line whose line shape is \ff{head} or \ff{hmethod} (with 0 horizontal
  arguments) becomes a \emph{vertical application} as soon as a deeper-indent
  child line appears.
Each deeper child at indent \(K+2\) is an argument of the head.

\begin{ffcode}
malloc.for > @           // head line: hmethod with 0 hargs + name
  0                      //   first vertical arg
  [m] >>                 //   second vertical arg (a master formation)
    body
\end{ffcode}

The name of a vertical application lives on the head line.
The outer kind is \ff{vapplication}.

\subsubsection{Vertical method chain}\label{sec:syntax:vmethod}

A head line of kind \ff{head}, \ff{hmethod} (with 0 hargs), or
  \ff{bare-formation} may be followed by a same-indent line beginning
  with \ff{.method}.
That continuation extends the expression into a \emph{vertical method chain}:
  one chain link per same-indent \ff{.method} line, in source order.

\begin{ffcode}
tmpdir                   // head line
.tmpfile                 //   continuation 1
.deleted                 //   continuation 2
.open > @                //   continuation 3, naming line
  "r"                    //   vapp arg of .open
  f > [f]                //   vapp arg of .open
\end{ffcode}

The name of the whole chain lives on the \emph{last} \ff{.method} line.
If that last \ff{.method} line also carries deeper-indent children,
  the chain's outer kind is \ff{vapplication} over a \ff{vmethod} head,
  and the naming line is still the last \ff{.method} line.

The outer kind is \ff{vmethod} when the last \ff{.method} carries no
  horizontal arguments, and \ff{vmethod-with-hargs} when it does;
  in the latter case the chain is closed for further extension.

\subsubsection{Vertical reversed dispatch}\label{sec:syntax:vreversed}

The vertical form of reversed dispatch is described in
  \cref{sec:syntax:reversed}.
After the receiver and method-argument children have been read, the outer
  kind remains \ff{bare-reversed} but its openness transitions to
  \emph{vertical-completed}: a same-indent \ff{.method} continuation may
  still wrap the expression
  (\cref{sec:wf:notation}).

\subsubsection{Compact tuple body}\label{sec:syntax:vcompact}

After the head line of a compact-tuple expression (\cref{sec:syntax:compact}),
  the deeper-indent body is a single block of children.
The first \(N\) children are direct positional arguments;
  the remaining children fold into the synthesised \(\Phi.\mathtt{tuple}\)
  argument that closes the head.
The outer kind remains \ff{compact-tuple}.

\subsubsection{Inline-phi formation}\label{sec:syntax:inline-phi}

A line carrying the suffix \ff{> [params] > name} (or its auto-named
  variant) is an \emph{inline-phi formation}.
Its body is the left-hand expression of the suffix, not a deeper-indent block;
  the line is therefore closed for deeper-indent children.
The construct may freely appear as a vertical argument of an enclosing
  expression --- its closure applies only to its own depth, not to whatever
  it nests inside.

\begin{ffcode}
foo                            // vapplication head
  a                            //   arg 1
  b > [x] > bar                //   arg 2: inline-phi-formation
                               //   with body=b, param=x, name=bar
\end{ffcode}

The outer kind is \ff{inline-phi-formation}.

\subsubsection{Multi-line text block}\label{sec:syntax:vtext}

A text block (\cref{sec:syntax:text}) spans multiple source lines but
  presents to the syntactic layer as a single multi-line expression.
After the closing \ff{"""} (and any same-line method chain and name suffix),
  the outer kind is \ff{text-block};
  its body is lexical content, not children of the expression.

\subsection{XMIR Output}\label{sec:syntax:xmir}

EO compilers emit a parse tree in \emph{XMIR}, an XML-based meta-representation
  of EO.
An XMIR document is an XML tree whose nodes are \ff{<o>} elements;
  each element carries an object's name, base reference, and source position.
The complete schema is given by the XSD at
  \href{https://www.eolang.org/XMIR.xsd}{\texttt{https://www.eolang.org/XMIR.xsd}};
  a human-readable description of element and attribute semantics is given
  at \href{https://www.eolang.org/XMIR.html}{\texttt{https://www.eolang.org/XMIR.html}}.

To illustrate the surface mapping, the source fragment

\begin{ffcode}
[] > foo
  bar > x
\end{ffcode}

produces the XMIR

\begin{ffcode}
<o name="foo" line="1" pos="0">
  <o name="x" base="bar" line="2" pos="2"/>
</o>
\end{ffcode}

where \ff{line} is 1-indexed and \ff{pos} is 0-indexed.
The \(\varphi\)-calculus translation of the same source is the subject of
  \cref{sec:semantics};
  XMIR is the on-disk realisation of that translation, and is used by
  downstream toolchains for code generation and static analysis.

\subsection{Outer Kinds}\label{sec:syntax:outer-kinds}

Every well-formed EO expression has an \emph{outer kind} ---
  the syntactic class of its outermost form.
The twelve outer kinds are:
\(\mathtt{head}\),
\(\mathtt{hmethod}\),
\(\mathtt{happlication}\),
\(\mathtt{bare\text{-}formation}\),
\(\mathtt{bare\text{-}reversed}\),
\(\mathtt{reversed\text{-}with\text{-}hargs}\),
\(\mathtt{compact\text{-}tuple}\),
\(\mathtt{inline\text{-}phi\text{-}formation}\),
\(\mathtt{vapplication}\),
\(\mathtt{vmethod}\),
\(\mathtt{vmethod\text{-}with\text{-}hargs}\), and
\(\mathtt{text\text{-}block}\).
The reference table in \cref{app:outer-kinds} pairs each kind with its
  cardinality, whether it admits deeper-indent children, and whether it
  may be wrapped by a same-indent method continuation.
The well-formedness rules of \cref{sec:wf:notation} are stated in terms
  of those properties.
